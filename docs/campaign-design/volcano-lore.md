# The Ashen Gauntlet: Volcano Lore Foundation

## Purpose

This document establishes the broad historical, ecological, and cultural foundation of the volcano that hosts the Ashen Gauntlet.
It is deliberately macro-level. Individual regions, passages, encounters, settlements, and scenes should derive from these layers without treating them as rigid geographic zones.

The volcano is an inhabited archaeological landscape rather than an arena built from nothing.
The organisers arrived last. They did not tame, govern, or meaningfully control the volcano. They seized and fortified only the Crucible at the summit, then turned the untouched mountain below into the Gauntlet by making contestants survive the natural and inhabited world that already existed there.

The mountain should therefore feel layered rather than themed.
A single route may move from jungle into a dwarven aqueduct, through a collapsed elven vault, into a natural burrow, and emerge beside a current kobold shrine.

## Foundational Principle

The volcano is a three-dimensional web formed by geology, ancient infrastructure, living ecosystems, forgotten magic, and modern occupation.
Physical altitude is not the same as campaign progression.
A route can descend far below a starting point and still advance contestants inward toward the Crucible.
Likewise, a team beginning high on a cliff ridge may be physically closer to the summit while having poor actual access to the inner volcano.

The history below should explain why the volcano contains so many irregular shortcuts, dead ends, vertical passages, hidden chambers, and overlapping cultures.
## The Buried Age: Before the Volcano

Before the mountain became a volcano, people already lived here.
The oldest settlements are so ancient that no surviving culture can name them with certainty.
They occupied fertile valleys, river settlements, cave sanctuaries, hill villages, and ceremonial sites around what was then a very different landscape.

When the first great eruptions began, entire communities were destroyed beneath ash, mud, stone, and volcanic debris.
Some were buried in moments. Others survived long enough to seal themselves inside temples, cellars, caves, or defensive structures that were later entombed beneath successive eruptions.

These buried places now exist far below younger layers of the mountain.
They may be reached only through collapse shafts, ancient burrows, broken wells, deep fissures, or passages opened by later civilizations.

Their dead do not always rest.
Contestants may encounter ghosts, shades, revenants, wights, specters, or stranger ancestral spirits whose languages and customs predate recorded history.
Some remain attached to ruined homes. Others guard forgotten shrines or repeat the final hours before the eruption.

This buried age should feel alien even compared with the elves and dwarves that came later.
Its relics are not automatically powerful, but they are among the oldest evidence that the land around the volcano has been inhabited far longer than anyone believed.
## The Primordial Volcano

The eruptions transformed the landscape and created the mountain that exists today.
Its magma chamber lies where the Elemental Planes of Earth and Fire press unusually close to the Material Plane.
Heat, mineral growth, tremors, steam pressure, and elemental manifestations therefore behave in ways ordinary geology cannot fully explain.

Over ages, magma tubes, subterranean rivers, steam chambers, fissures, and enormous caverns repeatedly opened, collapsed, and reconnected.
This natural circulation system became the first true spiderweb inside the volcano.

Elemental creatures established themselves in the deepest hot zones: magmins, fire snakes, salamanders, fire elementals, and other beings drawn to intense heat.
Xorn and similar creatures roam mineral-rich stone.

Burrowing creatures also reshape the mountain.
Umber hulks, ankhegs, bulettes, and far larger subterranean creatures may leave tunnels that later inhabitants repurpose.
Some passages used during the Games may be abandoned burrows hundreds of years old.
Others may still have living creators.

The scale of some tunnels should imply that creatures far beyond the contestants' ordinary experience still move beneath the mountain.
## The Hidden Elven Age

Thousands of years later, an elven civilization discovered that the volcano was more than a mountain.
They became geomancers, astronomers, magical natural philosophers, and custodians of the planar convergence beneath it.

They did not build one enormous city.
Instead, they established scattered hidden complexes at locations important to their studies: observatories, sanctuaries, botanical vaults, ritual chambers, research halls, and sealed archives.
Their sites may therefore appear in seemingly impossible places and at wildly different elevations.

Elven routes were not designed like ordinary roads.
They followed magical geometry, ley structures, pressure points, and planar alignments.
This explains some of the volcano's most surprising connections: a concealed passage on a ridge may eventually reach a deep internal sanctuary, while a chamber beneath a waterfall may connect to a much higher volcanic shelf.

Much of this infrastructure is now broken, dormant, concealed, or only partially understood.
Where it remains intact, its workmanship and magic can appear almost untouched despite the devastation surrounding it.
## Feral Elven Biomes

Some elven complexes were enclosed ecological reserves fed by geothermal warmth, magical light, and carefully redirected water.
After the civilization disappeared, these systems continued without their caretakers and became vast feral biomes.

A cavern may contain giant ferns, towering flowering plants, warm mist, underground rivers, mineral light, enormous trees, and grazing megafauna.
Different entrances may reach the same biome from the low jungle, a dwarven water system, a deep cave, or a high volcanic shelf.
The biome itself can therefore function as a major multi-entrance traversal region within the web.

Suitable inhabitants include giant elk, giant boars, giant apes, dinosaurs, giant insects, shambling mounds, and other oversized herbivores and predators.
Wyverns, giant eagles, manticores, or similar flying creatures may nest in the cavern heights.
The ecology should sometimes suggest creatures much larger than anything the party is expected to confront directly.

These spaces should initially feel impossible and natural, with their artificial origin revealed only through surviving elven structures, irrigation works, magical controls, or botanical records.
## Sphinx Vaults and Memory Labyrinths

The elves eventually learned things about the volcano that they did not wish to leave freely accessible.
Some knowledge was sealed in isolated vaults guarded by beings capable of judging those who reached them.
Sphinxes are natural custodians for these sites and should normally function as ancient judges, riddlers, witnesses, or keepers of truth rather than ordinary enemies.

A sphinx may remember civilizations, champions, contestants, and secrets that the modern organisers would prefer forgotten.
Such chambers can serve as rare pockets of stillness where the frantic competition temporarily gives way to ancient perspective.

The elves also built magical memory labyrinths: controlled testing environments intended to challenge initiates, researchers, or intruders through illusion, memory, repetition, and altered perception.
Earthquakes and magical decay have damaged some of them.
Their original tests now loop, misfire, trap memories, distort time, or preserve people long beyond any reasonable span.

A contestant lost many Games ago might believe only days have passed.
Shadows, specters, minotaurs, trapped spirits, or magical constructs may now inhabit these broken trials.
These labyrinths are particularly suitable as dangerous optional destinations whose rewards are knowledge, secrets, Favor, unusual routes, or access to forgotten infrastructure rather than simple upward progress.
## The Dwarven Age

Long after the elven decline, dwarven settlers rediscovered the volcano and used it for entirely different purposes.
Where the elves studied and stabilized the mountain, the dwarves mined, forged, transported, irrigated, fortified, and settled it.

Over centuries they created the closest thing the volcano has ever had to a coherent transportation network:
- Mine galleries and service tunnels.
- Great stairs and defensive gates.
- Geothermal forge halls.
- Aqueducts, reservoirs, and pumping stations.
- Bridges across fissures and caverns.
- Cargo lifts and enormous vertical shafts.
- Worker settlements and fortified halls.
- Cart roads and long lateral transport passages.

Dwarven infrastructure should form many of the most understandable connections in the campaign web.
A broken lift can connect several elevations.
An aqueduct can carry travellers around a large portion of the volcano.
A maintenance route can bypass a natural obstacle or connect two otherwise unrelated historical layers.

Their engineering also explains why some chambers remain pristine beside total ruin.
Exceptional masonry, magical craft, and deliberate reinforcement allowed isolated structures to survive disasters that destroyed everything around them.
## The Shattering

At some point the dwarves interfered with forces the elves had once contained or understood.
The exact cause should remain open for later design: over-mining, deliberate sabotage, an elemental breach, civil conflict, failed magical engineering, or some combination of these may all be possible interpretations.

What followed was not necessarily one instant apocalypse.
A prolonged era of eruptions, earthquakes, collapses, flooding, and magical instability may have broken the dwarven civilization over years or decades.

The result is the mixed physical language of the modern volcano:
- Perfect dwarven halls ending in collapsed stone.
- Natural lava tubes cutting through crafted tunnels.
- Mud and ash filling former roads.
- Elven complexes accidentally exposed by later collapses.
- Buried pre-volcanic ruins revealed beneath both.
- Steam vents and rivers redirected through abandoned infrastructure.

The Shattering is the main historical reason that orderly ancient systems have become an irregular three-dimensional labyrinth.
## The Living Ridge Cultures

The volcano was never truly abandoned.
Current peoples occupy the jungle belts, ridge systems, cliffs, caves, and sheltered volcanic terraces.
They should not be treated as arena monsters placed for contestants, but as communities with their own histories, rivalries, economies, sacred places, and opinions about the Games.

Possible inhabitants include orc clans, ogre families, hill giant households, and mixed communities that have adapted to the mountain over generations.
Some may resent the organisers. Others may profit from annual contestants through trade, guides, salvage, betting, tribute, or information.
Some consider the Games a desecration. Others regard them as just another seasonal hazard.

These cultures know routes that official maps do not: old hunting paths, hidden springs, safe ridge crossings, seasonal animal trails, dangerous vents, and forgotten entrances.
They can therefore influence traversal as much as combat.

## Kobold Networks and Dragon Cults

Kobolds occupy cracks, vents, abandoned service passages, furnace ducts, and other spaces too small or inconvenient for most larger inhabitants.
Rather than one centralized colony, the volcano should contain many clans with overlapping but distinct beliefs.

Most interpret the mountain through dragons and elemental fire.
One clan may worship a living dragon, another the volcano itself, another a great furnace, and another fire elementals believed to be fragments of a divine draconic ancestor.

Their tunnels form a secondary route network hidden inside the larger web.
Contestants who negotiate with them may gain access to shortcuts, ventilation shafts, forgotten doors, or routes beneath established paths.
Killing them indiscriminately can therefore have strategic consequences beyond the immediate encounter.

Dragons should remain rare but consequential.
A living red dragon, an abandoned lair, old eggshells, draconic relics, or territories shaped by previous dragons can influence entire regions without requiring dragons to become routine opponents.
## Deep Fire Societies

Below much of the dwarven infrastructure, elemental influence becomes stronger and more culturally important.
Firenewts, salamanders, azers, magmins, fire elementals, and related beings may occupy deep volcanic regions, old forge complexes, magma-adjacent caverns, and sealed ritual sites.

These peoples should not form one unified faction.
Some worship fire as divinity, some bind or control it, some consider the volcano a gateway, and some may believe that opening that gateway would bring transformation or ruin.
Others may be descendants or custodians of agreements made with the dwarves or elves.

This layer provides a meaningful downward route through the campaign.
Contestants may deliberately descend far beneath their starting elevation because deep passages can later connect inward or upward through shafts, geothermal systems, ancient lifts, or pressure tunnels.

## Exiles, Wizards, Shamans, and Hermits

The volcano attracts people who want isolation, forbidden knowledge, elemental power, or escape from ordinary society.
Crazed wizards, serious scholars, shamans, prophets, failed cult leaders, elemental researchers, and exiles can all plausibly occupy forgotten chambers.

Their homes should frequently appropriate older structures.
A wizard may use a dwarven observatory as a laboratory.
A shaman may occupy an elven geothermal control chamber.
A cult may have converted a buried village shrine without understanding its original purpose.

Not all such inhabitants are mad or hostile.
Some may know routes, history, or truths about the Games unavailable anywhere else.
Their isolation allows highly individual NPCs to exist without requiring entire supporting settlements.
## Previous Contestants

The Games have added another layer of inhabitants.
Contestants disappear every year, and official records do not necessarily distinguish between the dead, the missing, and those who deliberately remain hidden.

Over time this creates survivors, hermits, enclaves, fallen champions, and former teams that have adapted to the volcano.
One survivor may live among ridge clans, another lead kobolds, another inhabit a hidden dwarven hall, and another remain trapped inside an elven labyrinth while believing only days have passed.

Some previous contestants may know secrets about the Games, the organisers, the Crucible, or routes the organisers never discovered or never understood.
Their existence makes campaign history tangible and gives the volcano continuity across years of competition.

## The Organisers and the Games

The organisers arrived last and control almost nothing of the volcano itself.
Their true foothold is the Crucible: a fortified arena complex at the summit that they have occupied, reshaped, and made suitable for the final contest.
Everything below it remains nature, ruins, inhabitants, forgotten infrastructure, and dangerous territory the organisers neither govern nor fully understand.

The Ashen Gauntlet is therefore not a maintained obstacle course.
The challenge is reaching the Crucible at all.
Contestants must cross a living mountain whose routes can collapse, change, become occupied, or prove unknown even to those running the Games.

Contestants do not normally enter the volcano by road.
Teams travel toward public staging grounds in places such as Waterdeep, often by wagon and under the spectacle of departure ceremonies, and are then transported by organiser magic directly to assigned starting positions around the jungle and ridge systems.
Different teams may therefore begin at radically different elevations and sides of the volcano without requiring accessible roads between the outside world and those locations.

Each contestant bears a magical arena bracelet that binds them to the Gauntlet.
While the bracelet remains in place, ordinary escape or return from the arena is prevented; entry is effectively one-way.
The bracelets are removed only for those who survive the Gauntlet under its rules, making successful completion not merely victory but the normal means of regaining freedom from the arena.

The organisers may know fragments of the mountain through centuries of Games, reports from survivors, magical observation, and accumulated maps, but knowledge is not control.
A route can be known without being safe, observed without being reachable, or completely absent from organiser records.

## Bracelets, Observation, Spotlight, and Favor

The bracelets are also the magical anchor that allows the Games to follow contestants through the volcano.
The organisers can observe contestant activity throughout the Gauntlet even when the public is not currently watching that team. This continuous oversight is what allows Favor to respond to choices made anywhere in the mountain rather than only during broadcast moments.

Public attention works differently. When a team is placed under the Spotlight, the bracelets manifest or attract a magical observation orb near the contestants. Through that orb the audience can tune directly into the team and follow what they are doing.
The orb is therefore visible evidence that the contestants are currently performing before the crowd, not the source of the organisers' general ability to monitor the Games.

Favor remains active whether or not a team is under the Spotlight. Individual organisers and patrons can reward combat, mercy, exploration, spectacle, deception, cleverness, tradition, puzzles, or other behaviour according to their own values.
Spotlight amplifies those consequences: impressive actions performed before the audience are worth substantially more, while embarrassing, cowardly, wasteful, or simply uneventful use of valuable Spotlight attention may also create stronger negative reactions.

The precise numerical bonuses and penalties belong to the campaign systems design, but the lore principle is core: contestants are always being judged, while Spotlight creates moments when they know the entire audience is watching.

## Design Implications for the Volcano Web

Historical and ecological layers should overlap rather than becoming clean campaign zones.
A single node may contain several layers at once, and a route may move between them repeatedly.

The spiderweb should derive its irregularity from three broad sources:
1. Natural geology and ancient burrows.
2. Elven magical infrastructure and ecological systems.
3. Dwarven transportation, industrial systems, and later collapse.

Modern cultures, kobolds, elemental societies, exiles, and former contestants occupy and reinterpret this inherited network. The organisers remain largely external to it physically, touching the mountain chiefly through contestant insertion, bracelet-based observation, the Spotlight spectacle, and their stronghold at the Crucible.

As macro-regions are designed, each region should identify which historical layers are dominant, which are hidden, and which route technologies connect it to distant parts of the volcano.
The campaign should avoid a simple sequence such as jungle, ruins, caves, summit.
Instead, progression should feel like discovering how multiple histories and ecosystems interlock inside one vast mountain.
